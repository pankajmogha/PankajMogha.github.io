Organ-on-chip systems are engineered microphysiological platforms that recreate key structural, mechanical, biochemical, and cellular features of human tissues in vitro. They are needed because conventional two-dimensional cell culture and many animal models often fail to capture the complex tissue architecture, fluid flow, matrix mechanics, vascular interfaces, immune interactions, and disease-relevant microenvironments that shape human biology. By building controlled tissue models on microfluidic devices, organ-on-chip approaches can help study development, disease progression, drug response, toxicity, barrier function, vascularization, and cell-cell communication in a more physiologically relevant setting.

Current organ-on-chip technologies combine microfabrication, biomaterials, stem cell biology, primary cell culture, extracellular matrix engineering, live imaging, biosensors, and computational analysis. Common platforms include lung-on-chip, gut-on-chip, liver-on-chip, vascularized tissue chips, tumor-on-chip, and multi-organ systems. These models can incorporate perfusable channels, endothelial barriers, epithelial compartments, hydrogels, mechanical stretch, oxygen gradients, immune cells, and patient-derived cells. Advances in induced pluripotent stem cells, 3D bioprinting, organoids, and single-cell profiling are making these systems increasingly powerful for modeling human-specific biology.

Despite their promise, organ-on-chip models still face important limitations. Device fabrication and operation can be technically demanding, and protocols are not yet standardized across laboratories. Reproducing full tissue complexity remains difficult, especially for long-term maturation, innervation, immune integration, vascular perfusion, and organ-organ communication. Many platforms have low throughput compared with standard screening methods, and biological variability from primary or stem-cell-derived cells can complicate interpretation. Materials such as PDMS may absorb small molecules, which can affect drug testing.

Another challenge is validation. A chip model must be benchmarked against human tissue physiology, clinical data, and relevant disease phenotypes before it can be used confidently for mechanistic or translational studies. Scaling, reproducibility, sensor integration, data analysis, and regulatory acceptance remain active areas of development. This project focuses on building and applying organ-on-chip systems that better capture human tissue microenvironments for disease modeling, therapeutic discovery, and mechanistic studies.
