Cells do not respond only to soluble signals. They also sense the physical and mechanical properties of their microenvironment, including matrix stiffness, extracellular matrix composition, cell density, cell-cell forces, and tissue architecture. These mechanical cues influence stem cell maintenance, proliferation, differentiation, migration, senescence, and disease-relevant behavior.

This project brings together my work on human mesenchymal stem cells, keratinocytes, matrix elasticity, cell density, chemotaxis, and extracellular matrix coatings. My studies have examined how soft substrates can preserve proliferative and adipogenic potential during long-term stem cell expansion, how cell density can override substrate stiffness responses, how matrix elasticity regulates mesenchymal stem cell chemotaxis, and how ECM proteins such as gelatin can support stem cell expansion while reducing heterogeneity.

Mechanobiology is important because many cell culture systems fail to reproduce the physical cues that cells experience in vivo. Standard plastic culture surfaces are orders of magnitude stiffer than most tissues, which can distort cell behavior and obscure physiologically relevant mechanisms. Biomaterials and tunable hydrogel systems provide a way to model these physical features more carefully.

Key limitations remain. Mechanical effects are often context-dependent and can vary with cell source, passage number, matrix chemistry, ligand density, geometry, and culture duration. It is also difficult to separate stiffness effects from changes in adhesion, spreading, cytoskeletal organization, and paracrine signaling. Long-term culture adds further complexity because cells remodel their environment and change with time.

This project focuses on designing and using biomaterial platforms to understand how mechanical and extracellular matrix cues regulate stem cell behavior, tissue engineering outcomes, and regenerative potential.
