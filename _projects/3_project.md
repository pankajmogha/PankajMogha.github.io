Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics are transforming how we study complex tissues. Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals gene expression differences between individual cells, while spatial transcriptomics preserves information about where those cells or transcripts are located within tissue architecture. Together, these approaches help connect cellular identity, molecular state, neighborhood organization, and tissue function. They are especially important for understanding development, regeneration, disease progression, immune interactions, tumor microenvironments, and heterogeneity that is hidden in bulk measurements.

Current single-cell approaches include droplet-based sequencing, plate-based sequencing, single-nucleus RNA sequencing, multiome assays, lineage tracing, and perturbation screens. Spatial transcriptomics methods include array-based capture platforms, imaging-based methods such as MERFISH and seqFISH, and sequencing-based in situ approaches. These techniques can be integrated with histology, immunofluorescence, epigenomics, proteomics, and computational cell-type annotation to build molecular maps of tissues and disease models.

The major strength of single-cell sequencing is its ability to resolve diverse cell states and rare populations, but it usually loses spatial context during tissue dissociation. Dissociation can also introduce stress responses, selectively lose fragile cell types, and alter the apparent composition of a sample. Single-nucleus approaches can reduce some dissociation biases and work with frozen tissue, but they may capture different transcript populations compared with whole-cell methods.

Spatial transcriptomics preserves tissue organization, but current platforms vary widely in resolution, sensitivity, gene coverage, throughput, cost, and tissue compatibility. Some methods capture whole-transcriptome data but at multicellular resolution, while high-resolution imaging methods may profile targeted gene panels rather than the full transcriptome. Data analysis is also challenging because it requires segmentation, normalization, batch correction, cell-type deconvolution, spatial statistics, and integration with single-cell references.

This project focuses on using single-cell and spatial transcriptomic approaches to understand how cellular identity, tissue organization, and molecular programs interact in development and disease-relevant biological systems.
